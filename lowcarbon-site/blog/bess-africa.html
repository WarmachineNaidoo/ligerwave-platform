Battery Energy Storage: The Missing Link in Africa's Energy Transition

February 28, 2026 Storage Technology

For all the progress Africa has made in deploying renewable energy generation, one fundamental challenge remains: the sun does not always shine, and the wind does not always blow. Without a cost-effective way to store electricity when it is abundant and release it when it is needed, the continent's renewable energy ambition will hit a ceiling. Battery energy storage systems (BESS) are rapidly emerging as the technology that breaks through that ceiling.

The global energy storage market is experiencing explosive growth. According to BloombergNEF, global BESS deployments reached approximately 100 GWh in 2025, more than double the volume installed just two years prior. Africa, while currently a small fraction of this total, is positioned for accelerated uptake as costs decline and grid operators grapple with the integration of variable renewable energy sources.

The Economic Case for Storage

The economics of battery storage have transformed dramatically over the past five years. Lithium-ion battery pack prices have fallen below USD 100/kWh at the cell level, and complete turnkey BESS solutions — including power conversion systems, thermal management, and balance-of-plant — are now available at system prices below USD 250/kWh. At these price points, a 100 MW / 400 MWh BESS facility can be deployed for roughly USD 80-100 million, with a levelised cost of storage (LCOS) that competes with gas peaker plants for many applications.

For African electricity markets, where grid stability is often a significant challenge, the value proposition of BESS extends well beyond simple energy arbitrage. The key revenue and value streams include:

  • Grid frequency regulation: Batteries can respond to frequency deviations in milliseconds, far faster than conventional thermal plants. This makes them ideal for maintaining grid stability as more inverter-based renewable generation comes online.
  • Renewable firming: By co-locating storage with solar PV or wind farms, developers can smooth output variability and ensure a consistent power profile that matches off-taker requirements.
  • Load shifting: Energy generated during low-demand, high-solar periods can be stored and discharged during evening peaks, capturing higher electricity prices and reducing the need for expensive peaking capacity.
  • Transmission deferral: Strategically located BESS can reduce congestion on constrained transmission corridors, deferring or eliminating the need for expensive grid infrastructure upgrades.
"Battery storage is not just an enabler of renewable energy — it is a grid infrastructure asset in its own right. The question for African utilities is not whether to invest in storage, but how to integrate it optimally into system planning."

South Africa: A Bellwether Market

South Africa is emerging as Africa's leading BESS market, driven by the confluence of three factors: severe grid constraints, an ambitious renewable energy procurement programme, and a growing recognition that storage is a cost-effective solution to load-shedding. Eskom's flagship BESS projects at Hex and Skaapkraal, totalling over 200 MW / 800 MWh, represent some of the largest battery installations on the continent and serve as a proof of concept for utility-scale storage deployment in African conditions.

The private sector is also moving rapidly. Mining companies, which face both grid reliability challenges and pressure to decarbonise their operations, are increasingly investing in solar-plus-storage solutions. Gold Fields' South Deep mine, for example, operates one of the largest combined solar-storage installations in the country, demonstrating the commercial viability of the technology for industrial off-takers.

Technology Trends

Several technology trends are shaping the next generation of BESS deployments across Africa:

  • LFP chemistry dominance: Lithium iron phosphate (LFP) batteries have largely supplanted nickel-manganese-cobalt (NMC) chemistries for stationary storage applications, offering better cycle life, improved thermal safety, and lower cost — all critical for the demanding operating conditions of African deployments.
  • Longer duration systems: While 1-2 hour systems have dominated historically, 4-hour systems are now standard, and 6-8 hour systems are entering the market. These longer-duration systems enable true load shifting and can support grid stability through extended evening peaks.
  • Hybrid control systems: Advanced energy management system (EMS) software that optimises the interaction between solar generation, battery storage, and grid offtake is becoming a key differentiator for project value optimisation.
  • Second-life applications: As electric vehicle batteries reach end-of-life for automotive use, their repurposing for stationary storage applications offers a lower-cost entry point for smaller-scale deployments, though utility-scale applications continue to favour new-build systems for performance guarantees.

As battery costs continue to decline and African grids become increasingly renewable-rich, the role of energy storage will only grow. Battery energy storage is not merely complementary to renewable generation — it is the missing link that transforms variable renewable energy into a reliable, dispatchable power source capable of meeting Africa's growing electricity demand.
