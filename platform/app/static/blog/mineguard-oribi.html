The 30 Minutes That Shouldn't Have Been Silent

MineGuard • How a mining disaster shaped our zero-wearable safety system
June 2026 • 5 min read • Based on incident reports from Oribi Mine, 2020

At 10:17 AM on a Tuesday morning, a seismic event at Oribi Mine in South Africa's Mpumalanga province trapped three workers behind 40 tonnes of fallen rock. The rescue teams arrived within 20 minutes. They knew approximately where the men were. They did not know if they were alive.

The rescue operation took seven hours. Two men survived. One did not. The official incident report included a line that stayed with us: "No technology existed to confirm life signs through rock."

"No technology existed to confirm life signs through rock." — Oribi Mine incident report, 2020

Every mining safety system at the time required the miner to actively participate in their own safety. Wearable GPS trackers needed batteries. Gas detectors needed calibration. RFID badges needed to be worn. If a miner was unconscious, trapped, or simply forgot their device — the system had nothing to report.

MineGuard was built to solve this. Our technology uses WiFi Channel State Information (CSI) — the same radio signals that already flow through every tunnel — to detect human presence, heart rate, breathing, and movement through rock. No wearable. No battery. No badge.

When a miner is trapped behind a fall of ground, MineGuard can tell rescuers: two survivors with normal heart rates 15 metres in, one survivor 20 metres in with elevated HR. It cannot dig them out. But it tells the rescue team where to dig first.

Since the Oribi incident, three more miners have died in South African mines during the 2024-25 financial year alone. Every one of them was wearing a safety device. None of those devices transmitted through rock.

MineGuard doesn't replace safety protocols. It removes the gap between "I'm wearing a device" and "someone knows I'm here." That gap is what we set out to close.
